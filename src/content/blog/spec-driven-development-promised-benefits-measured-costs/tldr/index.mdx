Spec-driven development (SDD) means writing a structured specification before an AI coding agent starts work, and letting the agent turn it into a plan, a task list and code. One camp sees an expensive ritual that has not proven its promise; the other sees the obvious next step once agents write the code. **As of October 2026, SDD solves real problems, but it is not free. Its costs are measured; its benefits are mostly promised, and the evidence on both sides is thinner than either camp admits.** I use SDD in my own work and find it valuable, but this is a report, not a verdict.

- **The term is loose.** Birgitta Böckeler's three levels help: spec-first (a spec for one change, then dropped), spec-anchored (a spec kept and maintained with the code) and spec-as-source (humans edit only the spec). Most real use sits at the first level. The third has stalled: Tessl, its best-known champion, repositioned itself around agent skills. The lightest form is being absorbed into plan modes.
- **Much attention, modest maturity, no measured adoption.** Spec Kit, OpenSpec, Kiro, BMAD and others draw large star counts, but stars measure attention. Spec Kit's maintainer called its 1.0 release "an experiment, on purpose". Analysts are watching, yet no survey reports what share of teams use SDD.
- **The overhead is real and consistently reported.** In Colin Eberhardt's test, the agent ran for 33 minutes; review then took about three and a half hours, mostly for 2,577 lines of Markdown describing 689 lines of code. His iterative rebuild took about half an hour in total. In a five-reviewer pilot, reviewers with specifications took 48 minutes against 27 and found the same share of issues, though they could tie their findings to spec clauses. Tokens cut both ways: detailed specs cost tokens to write and can save tokens to execute.
- **Control can be an illusion.** Agents ignore instructions, and _phantom completions_, tasks marked as done but never implemented, now need their own detection tooling. In a study of spec files from 73,030 repositories, 81.5% had a single commit: little sign of the living specification that the spec-anchored level depends on. Kent Beck's objection is that SDD assumes you learn nothing during implementation. Delta specs, which describe only what one change adds, are a plausible answer, but not yet observed at scale.
- **The promise is argued mostly from principle.** "Code itself is actually a lossy projection from the specification," as Sean Grove put it. The measured evidence covers components, not the workflow: writing contracts first helped an agent catch 63.2% of bugs against 53.4%, and projects with better specifications spend fewer commits on bug fixes, an association that does not show cause. The one workflow pilot had no control group. Popular headline figures trace back to unrelated or uncheckable sources.
- **Specifications fix only one cause of failure.** In 20,574 real agent sessions, underspecified instructions caused 15.4% of misalignment episodes; agents not following instructions they had been given caused 36.5%. Verification still has to cover the rest.
- **The evidence is lopsided.** The overhead is mostly measured on small, solo, short-lived features, where supporters do not claim the payoff. The payoff is claimed for long-lived, team-scale work, where little has been measured. No independent controlled study compares a full SDD workflow with plan mode or iterative work.

If the evidence does not settle it, risk appetite and the shape of the work do. Two risks pull in opposite directions: a team that fears the agent getting it wrong has reason to want more specification; a team that fears the investment not paying off has reason to want less. Beyond that, the deciding factors are task size and clarity ("If you could describe the diff in one sentence, skip the plan," in Anthropic's words), how long the spec will live, how capable the model is (structured specs help weaker models most), how much review capacity the team has, and how strong its engineering practice already is. Regulated work, which already demands traceability, and brownfield systems, where specs work best near the area of change, shift the calculation.

Many land on a middle path: specifications scaled to the task, backed by executable checks, kept with the code and revised as the team learns. That is a recommendation, not a measured convergence. For teams already using plan mode, the open question is what a framework adds beyond a committed plan file.

Before adopting, ask which problem you are solving, who will keep the spec current, where your agent failures actually come from, and how you will know whether it worked, beyond self-reported speed. **Whether SDD pays depends on the work, the team and its appetite for two kinds of risk, and that judgement belongs to each team.**
