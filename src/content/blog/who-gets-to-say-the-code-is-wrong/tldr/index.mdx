A senior engineer who owns the code reviews a change from a newer colleague who writes in a second language, and leaves four words: _this is wrong, rewrite it_. The colleague goes quiet, and the reviewer reads the quiet as agreement. Each decides the other has a communication problem. Our industry's answer is "just be direct", but that quietly assumes you are allowed to be. **The argument we file under communication style is usually an argument about standing: who gets to tell whom that they are wrong.** And standing is decided before anyone types a word.

- **Radical candor is right, but it has an unwritten condition.** Kim Scott's idea is care and challenge together, and she rules out the ugly version: "It's not brutal honesty." The problem is what it assumes: that the room will read your challenge as help rather than as stepping out of line. Between equals, "this is wrong" is a normal note; from a senior to a newcomer, in public, it lands as something final.
- **Structure decides standing first.** Who reports to whom, who owns the code, and whose name is on the design document decide whose challenge counts, and you can read them off a team before any argument begins. Anicich and colleagues found that power without status - authority over people who do not respect you - reliably produces conflict, with no cultural difference needed.
- **Language hands out standing unevenly.** A study of second-language speakers in global software teams found that the stronger speaker can shut down the weaker one's input without meaning to. The engineer carefully building a correct sentence is not less able, only harder to hear in that moment.
- **Culture comes last, and it is a hint, not a label.** Power distance and face shape who challenges upward and what a public correction costs. But Smith and Bond note that the dimension scores do not predict any individual, Fischer and Schwartz found that colleagues from the same background usually differ more than backgrounds do on average, and McSweeney doubted that culture causes anything systematically. Among globally networked engineers, national-culture differences are small and still converging, so structure and language are what remain. Read the relationship, not the category.
- **"Just be direct" is a one-sided licence.** The senior engineer is blunt and it reads as integrity; the newer one is blunt in the same way and it reads as difficult. One pays nothing for directness, the other pays in how much room they get next time. The sides are not fixed: the code owner here is the outsider in the next repository. Scott herself says feedback is "measured at the listener's ear, not at the speaker's mouth", and Erin Meyer shows there is no neutral directness: every style comes from somewhere. The target is not radical candor but its wall-poster version.
- **Automate the standard, be careful with the person.** A shared, automatic standard such as coding conventions turns "your code is wrong" into "this does not match the rule we all agreed on". Objective standards for the code are impersonal and good; a blunt feedback style still lands on a human being.
- **Psychological safety is what candor was after.** In Bresman and Edmondson's study of sixty-two drug-development teams, diversity slightly hurt performance on average but helped it in teams with high psychological safety. In software, Verwijs and Russo found that safety helped but did not neatly explain the link between diversity and results. The upside is possible, not promised: different views help only when they reach the table, and whether they do is a question of standing.
- **Friction pays only when it stays about the work.** De Wit, Greer and Jehn's meta-analysis found that arguing about the work improves decisions only when it stays separate from friction between people, which is uncommon. Shared standing keeps the two apart: the newer engineer can answer "here is why I did it this way" and be read as a colleague, not as difficult.

**When only the people in charge are allowed to push back, the friction doesn’t create innovation. It just lets power pass itself off as honesty.** The powerful stay direct, everyone else stays quiet, and the team mistakes that quiet for agreement - until people learn that objecting never changes anything.

So when a review goes wrong, don't ask who was too blunt or too sensitive. Ask who on this team cannot yet say "this is wrong" and be heard - and what decided that: the reporting line, the language, or whose code it is. Ask it of yourself too: where do you still owe someone the room to tell you that you are wrong? Style is the surface. Standing is the thing underneath it.
